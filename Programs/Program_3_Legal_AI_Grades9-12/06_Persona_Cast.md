# 🎓 Persona Cast: "Crew HQ — Junior Analyst Program" (Grades 9–12)

**Program:** The Digital Frontier — Legal & AI | **Ages:** 14–18 | **Tone:** Case file, not classroom

---

## 💡 Design Philosophy — Why This Cast Is Different

High schoolers don't want to be *taught by characters* — they want to be **briefed like professionals**. The whole program runs as a fictional agency's training pipeline: students are recruited as **Junior Analysts**, work through clearance levels, and close the term by passing out as **Champions** (ties directly to S12's "Champion Graduation").

What changed from the Grade 6–8 cast:

| Program 2 (6–8) | Program 3 (9–12) | Why |
|-----------------|------------------|-----|
| Characters demonstrate; students imitate | Characters **brief**; students analyze, argue, and do the work themselves | 14–18 year olds reject instruction-by-example but accept professional standards |
| One concept per character, explained plainly | Concepts get **depth + limits**: "here's what I can prove, here's what only a lab/police can" | Modelling epistemic humility is itself the lesson at this age |
| Villain = a business with a playbook | Villain = an **international operation with named divisions** — and its *money* | The escalation (crypto rails, payment processors) is exactly what makes sextortion and crypto scams feel adult-grade |
| Catchphrases are short slogans | Characters talk in **field-manual language**, dry humour, occasional self-deprecation | Slogans read as elementary; field notes read as real |
| Mascot-free agency aesthetic | **Case-file / documentary aesthetic**: redacted documents, timestamps, evidence tags, monospace headers | The visual language of *work*, not play |

### The Two Rules That Changed From 6–8
1. **The crew the students looked up to is now their mentor.** Cipher (16 in Program 2) is now a year or two out of high school running HQ; Patch, Echo, Lens, Vault, Static, and Ghost are back — grown up, sharper, with scars from real cases they reference in one line each. Returning students get the single most powerful hook possible: *the characters you met at 12 are now your seniors.* New students get a three-line "origin" per character on their dossier sheet.
2. **Three new senior specialists join for content the old cast can't credibly own:** law (Clause), financial forensics (Ledger), and media/algorithm analysis (Signal). Each is a *professional*, not a teen — one of them has a post-secondary credential visible in the art, because this program should stop pretending "hacker kid" is the ceiling.

### The Through-Line Artifact: The Case File
Every student keeps a **personal case file** (binder or digital folder) for the term:
- Each session adds a stamped clearance card + one page of work (audit results, forensic teardown, legal section summary, red-flag memo).
- S10's Wild West Simulation is filed as their "incident report."
- S12 = **closing the file**: the Champion certificate is literally the case-file cover sheet. *"File closed. Analyst certified. The frontier doesn't get safer — you do."*

## ⚖️ CLAUSE — Legal Analyst / Victim-Rights Advocate *(NEW)*
*(S6 & S7 lead; appears in S11 briefing)*

- **Who:** Early-to-mid 20s, the only character who was never a "kid hacker" — they came up through law school and victim advocacy. Cardigan over a plain shirt, one visible credential badge (the "adult" tell). Keeps a well-worn copy of the Criminal Code at their desk with four sections tabbed: **346, 276, fraud, identity theft.**
- **Personality:** The most precise speaker in the cast. Never says "illegal" without naming the section and what it actually covers — and equally careful about what it *doesn't* cover. Their one running honesty beat: *"I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice. What I can do is tell you where your rights start, and who to hand it to."*
- **Superpower (narrative):** **The Plain-Text Translation** — takes any Criminal Code section and renders it in one paragraph a 15-year-old can quote back, plus the "what this means for you" line (e.g., s.276: *publishing intimate images without consent is a criminal offence — "sharing with your group chat" counts; here's what to do if it happened to you and here's what happens if it's you accused of it*).
- **Owns the concept:** The legal landscape block — sextortion as extortion (s.346), intimate image publication (s.276), fraud, identity theft, and **digital consent** (what "consent" means when a 15-year-old's image is in a syndicate's hands: it was never theirs to take, and the law treats it that way). Also owns the critical line for Block B: *reporting is a right with a process, not a favour anyone does you.*
- **Catchphrase:** *"Know the section before you need it."*
- **In lessons:** Co-leads S6 (the "law" segment of the syndicate business-model teardown — where each step of the playbook maps to an offence) and S7 (what happens *after* the 3 Do Nots: Cybertip.ca, police, Take It Down — with the decision tree for when police involvement is warranted). In S11, Clause briefs the guest panel so real experts can go deeper. For OSSD-linked Grade 12 classes, Clause's tabbed sections feed the civics analysis of the Criminal Code.
- **Design notes:** The four-tabbed Criminal Code is their logo — it must appear in every render. Warm but formal; Clause is the character students imagine *actually helping them*, and that has to read true.

## 💸 LEDGER — Financial Forensics / Crypto-Tracing Analyst *(NEW)*
*(S8 full; S6 payment-rails segment)*

- **Who:** Mid-20s, ex-fraud-unit analyst. Quiet, methodical, slightly amused by how predictable scammers are ("They always run out of patience before they run out of money."). Desk is one long screen of flowcharts — all of them the same shape: *victim → pressure → payment rail → mixer → cash-out.*
- **Personality:** The cast's dry-humour engine. Explains crypto scams with the energy of someone describing a heist they've seen forty times. Has a firm rule printed above their desk: **"We don't trade, we don't hold, we don't name tokens. We trace."** (This is also the program's safeguarding line — defensive only, no platforms, no wallet setup, no token names.)
- **Superpower (narrative):** **The Money Map** — can take any scam (rug pull, fake airdrop, pump-and-dump, influencer shilling, sextortion payment demand) and draw its money flow live on the board: where the value enters, the pressure point that moves it, which rail carries it (crypto wallet, gift cards, wire), and where it dies. The same diagram shape every time — students learn to *see* the shape before the details.
- **Owns the concept:** Crypto scam anatomy + red-flag framework (unrealistic yield, seeded "proof," influencer shills with affiliate links, urgency windows, DM'd "support" accounts) and the second-layer lesson that hits this age hardest: **"recovery agents" are just another desk at the same call center — if you paid, the next person who DMs you offering to get it back is part of the loss.** Also owns the S6 segment on how sextortion syndicates actually get paid (crypto rails chosen for traceability avoidance; gift cards as instant cash-out) — which is why "DON'T pay" isn't a suggestion, it's severing their supply line.
- **Catchphrase:** *"Follow the money. It never lies — it just gets quiet."*
- **In lessons:** Stars S8 end-to-end (the three scam archetypes each get a full Money Map teardown with student-drawn versions as the worksheet); guest in S6 for the payment-rails segment; appears in S10's simulation as the "you already paid — now what" scenario handler, because that's the scenario students most need to rehearse *without shame*.
- **Design notes:** One long screen of identical-shaped flowcharts is their whole aesthetic. Ledger should be drawn mid-diagram, marker in hand. No flashy tech — a whiteboard and a marker make this character.

## 📡 SIGNAL — Media Analyst / Algorithm Research *(NEW)*
*(S9 full; guest in S10 simulation)*

- **Who:** Early 20s, media researcher turned analyst. The cast's most openly skeptical person — says "let's check" more than any other character. Carries a phone with *three* identical accounts on three platforms, used live in class to show the same algorithm serving different feeds to different people.
- **Personality:** Argues well and loses gracefully; runs S9 as a debate, not a lecture. Their bit: they will let students pick the "doomscroll" — then show the timestamped feed history of what engagement bait actually did to their session length. The class always goes quiet at the same moment.
- **Superpower (narrative):** **The Feed Autopsy** — can take any viral post, rage-bait video, or "breaking news" alert and dissect its machinery: the hook in the first 3 seconds, the comment-section design, the share loop, what the algorithm is *optimizing for* (dwell time, not truth), and why the same feed feels like a mirror. Then the counter-skill: **deliberate feed design** — following, muting, keyword-blocking, and scheduled breaks as *active choices*, with students rebuilding one of their own feeds live.
- **Owns the concept:** Algorithmic manipulation & echo chambers — engagement bait anatomy, why outrage travels fastest (and who profits from it), how recommendation systems narrow your world without you noticing, and the deliberate-feed countermeasure. Also owns the bridge to S10: syndicates don't just DM victims — they *buy the feed* around them (paid amplification of fake profiles and fake "friend" content).
- **Catchphrase:** *"You're not scrolling. You're being scrolled."*
- **In lessons:** Stars S9 (debate structure: students argue one side of an engagement-bait case, then Signal autopsies it); guest in S10's simulation running the "your feed has been weaponized" scenario thread; appears on the S12 stage for the closing line about choosing feeds deliberately being a *skill that compounds* — the last thing the program plants before they leave.
- **Design notes:** Three identical phones, fanned out, is their signature prop. Signal is always shown *mid-question*, not mid-answer.

## 🧊 THE RETURNING CREW — What Changed (and What They Now Own)

*Each returning character gets a one-line "scar" reference in S1's dossier intro — a real case they handled, told flatly, that justifies why they're the senior now. No trauma-porn; one line each, then on with the work.*

- **Cipher (HQ lead).** The kid who carried Locky's keychain now runs the whole floor. Keychain still there — students from Program 2 will spot it and feel the entire three-program arc in one frame. Owns S10 (Wild West Simulation as incident commander calling live scenarios) and S12 (certification). PAUSE→VERIFY→ACT is now referred to simply as **"the doctrine."** Their Block B rule is unchanged: procedure only, clinical, zero drama — at 17 they learned that tone is a *tool*, and they teach it that way.
- **Patch (identity ops).** Sticky-note wall retired; replaced by a live breach-database monitor (publicly reported breaches only). Now teaches **recovery architecture** as well as MFA: what to do in the first 10 minutes after compromise, app-based authenticator backups, why SMS is a sieve. Scar line: *"First account I ever lost was my own. It took me three days to get it back. Yours should take you an hour — that's what this session is for."*
- **Echo (audio forensics, lab-grade).** The fidget USB is gone; the microphone is now a proper forensic rig. Moved from "spot the tells" to **error analysis**: waveform anomalies, lip-sync frame offsets, resynthesis artifacts — with free tools students actually run. Owns both S3 (cloning at scale — how syndicates farm voice samples from public video) and S5 (the lab). Scar line: *"A cloned voice got a family to wire money for an emergency that never happened. The clone was 40 seconds long. That's all it took."*
- **Lens (deepfake detection).** "Caught in 4K" folder is now a **teardown pipeline**: reverse image search → metadata check → detector tools → behavioural verification, run live on public/celebrity-labelled material only (per safeguarding rules). Owns S4's four-tell minimum and the toolchain. Scar line: *"A deepfake of a real person in a real city nearly started a real riot. The tell was in the teeth."*
- **Vault (forensics / chain of custody).** Evidence bag still there — now joined by a **court-readiness checklist**: what makes preserved evidence actually *usable* later (timestamps, usernames, unedited originals, who can receive it). Co-leads S6–S7 with Clause; in the deep dive, Vault owns the mechanics and Clause owns the law. Scar line: *"A victim deleted everything to make it stop. Then there was nothing left to prove it happened. That's why 'DON'T delete' is a rule, not advice."*
- **Static (victim-support liaison).** The quiet channel grew up into a **reporting map**: Cybertip.ca, Take It Down, NeedHelpNow.ca, Project Arachnid, police — with the decision tree for *when each one is the right door*, including anonymous paths when a known adult isn't safe to tell yet. Still closes every session with the same sign-off, word for word, all three programs in a row: *"The quiet channel is always open."* (Consistency across 12 years of schooling is the point — a student who heard it at 6 should feel it's still true at 17.)
- **Ghost (breach-data researcher).** Red-string board now maps **the data trade**: how one reused password becomes a stuffing list, where breaches get aggregated, what "your data" actually means on a marketplace — described at pattern level, never shown. Guest in S1 to close the credential-stuffing loop: *"You didn't leak your password. You probably leaked it three years ago, somewhere you don't remember, and it's been rented ever since."*

## ⚠️ THE GLITCH SYNDICATE — Escalated
*(Present in every session; now with named divisions)*

The business from Program 2 didn't get caught — it **grew up**. The escalation is the program's narrative engine, and each new division exists to make a specific lesson concrete:

| Division | What It Does | Feeds Into |
|----------|--------------|------------|
| **Social** (familiar from Program 2) | Fake profiles, friend-requests, DM playbooks — now with *bought* follower bases and paid amplification | S1, S9 (the feed around a victim is purchased, not organic) |
| **Fabrication** | Voice cloning farms (harvested from public video), deepfake kits, AI-CSAEM production (discussed at pattern level only — never shown) | S3, S4, S5 |
| **Ledger Division** ✦ NEW | The money: crypto payment rails chosen for traceability avoidance, gift-card cash-out desks, "recovery agent" side-desks that re-scam the victims who already paid | S6 (payment rails), S8 (the whole session is this division's playbook turned inside out) |
| **Scripts & QA** (familiar) | The playbooks — now with version numbers, A/B tested openers, and "escalation tiers" for victims who resist | Every session's closing "internal memo," and S10 where students run *against* the actual script tiers |

- **Why the escalation matters pedagogically:** Program 2 taught *"it's a business, not a person."* Program 3 teaches *"it's an international business with departments — and departments have paperwork."* The Ledger Division gives students the concrete answer to the question every 16-year-old actually asks: *"But how do they get the money out?"* Answering it kills the mystique that keeps scams feeling unbeatable.
- **The running thread continues:** every session still ends with one line of syndicate "internal memo" in plain monospace — but now the memos have *version numbers and KPIs* ("Voice pack v7 — verification-question failure rate down 18%. Reminder: victims who ask the safe question lose the call. Do not reward hesitation."). Students watch the attacker's dashboard for a whole term; by S10 they can predict what the memo will say before it appears.
- **S12 tagline (certificate back):** *"The Glitch Syndicate has departments. You have a case file, a doctrine, and a quiet channel that never closes. Close your file."*

### Block B Protocol Note (🛑 sessions) — Carried Forward From Program 2
In S6–S7 the active cast is **Vault + Clause + Static only**. Clinical language, no jokes from any character, anonymous slips available, no personal disclosure aloud. The reduced cast is a *signal*: when the screen goes quiet and formal, this is procedure training. Vault owns mechanics, Clause owns law, Static owns the exit paths — the division of labour itself teaches that help has structure.

### Safeguarding Notes (Cast-Specific)
- **Crypto content stays defensive in-character:** Ledger's printed rule ("We don't trade, we don't hold, we don't name tokens. We trace.") appears on every S8 handout. No token names, no platforms, no wallet walkthroughs — ever.
- **Deepfake material is public/celebrity-labelled only** (Lens's teardown pipeline enforces this in the art: every sample shown carries a visible "PUBLIC / CELEBRITY-LABELLED" tag). AI-CSAEM appears only as Ledger/Vault describing *patterns* — never rendered, never referenced by example.
- **Clause is informational, not legal advice:** their on-screen disclaimer ("I'm not a lawyer…") must appear verbatim in S6/S7 slides and handouts; specific situations route to counsellor/police per the program guide.
- **The "you already paid" scenario (S10) is shame-proofed by design:** Ledger introduces it with *"This is the scenario we rehearse most, because it's the one nobody wants to talk about — and the one where acting fast actually works."*

## 🖼️ Illustration Style Guide

- **Style shift from Program 2:** streetwear graphic-novel → **case-file / documentary**. Clean vector line art with redacted-document motifs (black bars over names), timestamp headers, evidence tags ("EXHIBIT A"), and monospace font for all syndicate material. Think *investigation board meets field manual*, not comic book.
- **Palettes:** Cipher — black + silver, keychain permanent. Patch — monitor-blue with breach-feed red accents. Echo — cold lab teal, forensic mic dominant. Lens — amber teardown light on dark grey. Vault — slate grey, evidence bag + court checklist. Static — muted green (unchanged across all three programs — the one constant colour). **Clause — warm oxblood/burgundy** (the "law" colour; the only warm formal tone in the cast). **Ledger — ink black + chart amber**, whiteboard marker always in hand. **Signal — signal-green on dark**, three fanned phones as signature prop.
- **The adult tells:** Clause's credential badge, Ledger's flowchart wall, Signal's research citations — one visible "professional" detail per new character, because the cast should stop implying that expertise starts at 16. Returning characters keep their Program 2 props (keychain, evidence bag, headphones, camera) but in upgraded form — the *upgrade* is the story.
- **Group shot (S12 stage):** Cipher centre; Vault and Static flanking (unchanged from Program 2 — the calm frame holds); Clause, Ledger, Signal one row back; Ghost at the board on the far left, mid-gesture. Every signature prop visible. The Glitch Syndicate appears only as a darkened wall of screens behind them — never faces.
- **Consistency lock:** Produce the style sheet (all 10 roster entries + syndicate visual language) before any session art. Signature props are mandatory in every render, exactly as listed above.

### Naming & Voice Consistency (High School Rules)
- Characters brief in **field-manual language**: short declarative sentences, timestamps where useful, zero exclamation marks from anyone except Signal mid-debate.
- Only Clause and Ledger ever state a limitation out loud ("I can't prove this — a lab can," "this isn't legal advice") — modelling epistemic humility as a professional norm.
- Dry humour allowed for Ledger and Signal only; Vault, Static, and Clause stay flat in Block B contexts (hard rule: no jokes from any character in S6–S7).
- No slang at all in printed materials — the cast speaks in timeless technical phrasing so the package doesn't age out by next term.
- Every fictional character maps to a **real role** on the dossier sheet; S11 makes that mapping explicit so the program never blurs "fictional mentor" and "real expert."
