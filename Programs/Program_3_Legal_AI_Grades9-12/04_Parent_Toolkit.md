# 👨‍👩‍👧 Program 3 — Parent & Guardian Toolkit (Grades 9–12)

---

## Why This Age Is Different
Your teen is in the **Wild West**: adult-grade threats (credential stuffing, voice clones, organized sextortion syndicates, crypto scams) with adult consequences. And they've earned real privacy — so your role shifts from *monitor* to **backup node**. You're no longer checking over their shoulder; you're the person their protocol points to when something breaks. **Your job this program: be a reliable backup — and make sure they know the door stays open.**

> Note: some of these conversations are less about control and more about being *reachable*. Everything in this toolkit works only if your teen believes you'll be calm. Test it with a small story before you need it for real.

## The Three Family Agreements
1. **The No-Blame Promise (v2) — "the door stays open"** (say it out loud, to their face):
   *"If anything online ever gets you in trouble — even something that feels embarrassing or scary to tell me — I want to hear it from YOU first. I will not yell, and I'm not taking your phone as punishment. My job is to help you fix it."* Sign it together (p.4). Expect them to test this promise with a small story before the real one comes — honour the promise on the small test, or they won't trust it on the big one.
2. **The Safe Word + Callback Number** (from handout D3) — a word only your family knows, plus a rule: any urgent request by voice or text, especially money, without the safe word → don't act; call back a *known* number, never the caller's. Works both directions: scammers now clone **your teen's** voice to target you and grandparents.
3. **The No-OTP Rule** — nobody in this family shares one-time codes with anyone, including "bank" or "tech support" callers. Adult accounts get app-based MFA too: your email is your master key, and it's a top scammer target.

## If Your Teen Discloses Something (the most important page in this toolkit)
**Their first 24 hours matter more than any investigation.**
1. **No blame, no anger, no device seizure as punishment.** Say: "Thank you for telling me. This is not your fault. Here's what we'll do."
2. **Preserve evidence:** don't delete conversations or accounts; screenshot everything (dates, usernames, messages). If something was already deleted, log what can still be recovered — never reconstruct or exaggerate evidence.
3. **The 3 Do Nots apply to them too:** don't pay anything, don't reply to the scammer, don't let them feel they must fix it alone.
4. **The reporting decision tree:** images of a minor or threats to safety → **police now**; everything else → **Cybertip.ca first** (confidential, Canada's national tipline). Intimate images of minors: also **Take It Down** and **Project Arachnid**. They can report themselves — walk it with them if they want.
5. **The legal reality, said out loud:** the crime is committed by the threatener, every single time (extortion s.346; publication of intimate images s.276). Your teen is a victim, not a suspect. Say that sentence to their face.
6. **Support over surveillance:** temporary changes framed as care, not punishment. Watch for trauma responses (sleep changes, withdrawal, grade drops) — **NeedHelpNow.ca** is 24/7 and anonymous; follow up with the counsellor if symptoms persist a week or more.
7. **If you suspect your teen is on the OTHER side** (pressuring others): this is also a disclosure to handle calmly — often peer pressure or curiosity gone wrong. Guidance counsellor + C3P's restorative approaches exist for exactly this; shame guarantees it continues in secret.

## Quick Reference: The Adult's Cheat Sheet
- **Sextortion demand?** → No pay, no reply, screenshot, Cybertip.ca, Take It Down if images of a minor. Victim is never at fault.
- **"Is this really [your teen]?" voice/text asking for money** (grandparent calls YOU) → safe word; callback to known number; never send on urgency alone.
- **Shocking video/image of a real person** → assume it *might* be fake until verified; don't share while checking; if intimate and real → police + Take It Down.
- **Account hacked / phishing link clicked** → reset that password everywhere it's used, check MFA, review what the account held, warn their friends. Acting fast usually means no damage — breathe.
- **A "recovery agent" contacts them about lost money** → second-layer scam; ignore AND report to Cybertip.ca.
- **They've been talked into a crypto investment** → don't panic-yell (they'll hide it); ask what happened; if money is gone, bank + police fraud unit fast.
