# Phase 4 Plan: Grades 9–12 (Ages 14–18)
## "The Wild West: Con Artists, Crypto & AI"

> *The internet rewards the informed and punishes the impulsive. Think before you click, invest, or share.*

---

### 📋 Plan Overview

| Detail | Information |
|--------|-------------|
| **Duration** | 12 sessions × 50 minutes (or 6 × 100-min blocks) |
| **Delivery** | Classroom-based with computer lab access; can include guest speakers from local tech/cyber companies |
| **Materials Needed** | Computers/tablets, projectors, printed handouts, crypto simulation tokens, deepfake detection tools |
| **Prerequisites** | Familiarity with social media, at least one online game, and basic understanding of email and web browsing |
| **Cross-Curricular Links** | Computer Science (encryption, blockchain), Math (probability, data analysis), Economics (supply/demand in crypto), English (persuasive writing) |

---

### 🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this phase, students will be able to:
1. Audit personal accounts for security settings and identify password reuse across platforms
2. Set up app-based MFA and backup recovery methods
3. Detect deepfake images and audio using visual/auditory forensics clues
4. Analyze sextortion syndicates' business models and apply discreet reporting protocols
5. Evaluate crypto projects for legitimacy and spot common scam patterns (rug pulls, airdrops)
6. Understand algorithmic bias and engagement bait on social media platforms

### 📅 Session Breakdown

#### Session 1: "The Password Audit" — Credential Stuffing & Reuse (50 min)

**Objective:** Identify password reuse across accounts and understand credential stuffing attacks

| Time | Activity | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| 0-5 min | Hook | Show a headline: "Student's Instagram Hacked After Using Same Password as Leaked Gym Website." Discuss how one weak link breaks the chain. |
| 5-12 min | Direct Instruction | Explain credential stuffing: hackers take leaked username/password pairs and try them on other sites. Show statistics on password reuse rates (70%+ of people reuse passwords). |
| 12-22 min | Guided Practice: "My Password Map" | Students map their most-used accounts **by name only — never the password itself** — and mark each as reused (red) or unique (green). The point of the exercise is seeing *where* reuse exists, not recording *what* any secret is. For the hands-on half, students work a teacher-supplied **sample credential dump of fictional accounts** and find the single reused passphrase that unlocks two of them, so the detection skill is practised on material that cannot expose anyone. |
| 22-32 min | Group Activity: "The Weak Link Chain" | In groups, students build a paper chain where each link is an account. If one link breaks (weak/reused password), the whole chain falls. Discuss real-world examples. |
| 32-50 min | Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket | Students answer: "How many accounts share a password? What's one app you'd prioritize changing first? Why?" |

**Materials:** Projector, sample headlines, paper for account maps (account names only), teacher-supplied fictional credential dump worksheet, colored markers, paper chains or string/tape for chain activity

> **Non-negotiable:** no student writes a real password on paper. The map records
> account names and reuse patterns only. This matches the rule stated in the
> framework itself — *never real passwords, credentials, or personal information* —
> and the Program 3 worksheet, which asks for "the SITES, not the passwords."

#### Session 2: "MFA Mastery" — App-Based Authentication & Backups (50 min)

**Objective:** Set up authenticator apps and understand backup/recovery methods

| Time | Activity | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| 0-5 min | Hook | Compare SMS codes (sent over cellular network, vulnerable to SIM swap) vs. app-based codes (generated locally, more secure). Show a video of a SIM swap attack. |
| 5-12 min | Direct Instruction | Teach MFA setup: Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator, Authy. Explain backup methods: recovery codes, backup email, secondary phone number. |
| 12-22 min | Guided Practice: "Set Up Your Authenticator" | Students install an authenticator app on their phones/tablets and set up MFA for Google or Discord. Teacher circulates to assist. |
| 22-32 min | Group Activity: "Backup Plan" | Groups create a backup strategy: Where do they store recovery codes? What's their secondary email? How do they handle a lost phone? |
| 32-50 min | Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket | Students write: "Why is app-based MFA better than SMS? Name two backup methods and explain when each is useful." |

**Materials:** Projector, sample setup screens, tablets/computers with internet, authenticator app QR codes for scanning, blank paper

#### Session 3: "The Clone Wars" — Voice Cloning for Parents & Teens (50 min)

**Objective:** Verify identity when a call or voice note might be a clone, using an out-of-band check rather than listening for tells

| Time | Activity | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| 0-5 min | Hook | Play two audio clips: one real parent leaving a voicemail, one AI-cloned parent saying "It's me, I'm at the airport, call me back!" Students vote on which is real. Then reveal the answer and the point: most classes split roughly 50/50, and that is not a failure of attention — it is why ears are not a security control. |
| 5-12 min | Direct Instruction | Explain voice cloning: a convincing clone can be built from roughly **30 seconds of clear speech**, sometimes less, and most people have published far more than that. Show examples of scams targeting parents (fake emergency calls, bail money requests). The defence is procedural, not perceptual: agree a safe word in advance, and verify by calling back on a number you already have. |
| 12-22 min | Activity: "Family Safe Word Workshop" | Students draft safe words with family input. Practice using them in different contexts ("What's the capital of Mars?" vs. "What did we have for breakfast?" — the second is better: it is unguessable from a feed). Discuss storing safe words securely and changing one if it has been used online. |
| 22-32 min | Role-Play: "The Clone Call Scenarios" | In pairs, students role-play scenarios: (1) Parent calling from hospital, (2) Sibling asking for money, (3) Friend with cloned voice. They practice verifying identity, including the case where the caller *does not know the safe word* — hang up and call back on a known number. |
| 32-50 min | Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket | Students write: "What makes a strong safe word? What do you do if the caller fails the check — or refuses to do it?" |

**Materials:** Audio clips (licensed or public-domain recordings, or clips the teacher generated from synthetic voices — **do not record students or their families**), scenario cards, blank paper for drafting safe words, timers

#### Session 4: "Deepfake Detection Lab" — Visual Forensics (50 min)

**Objective:** Judge whether an image can be trusted using provenance first, and state the limits of the evidence

| Time | Activity | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| 0-5 min | Hook | Show a viral deepfake celebrity photo. Before anyone hunts for clues, ask: *who published this, when, and does any independent source show the same moment?* |
| 5-12 min | Direct Instruction | Teach the checks in order of strength: (1) **Source** — who posted it, on what account, and is that account verifiable; (2) **Reverse image search** — does the image appear earlier, elsewhere; (3) **Corroboration** — does anyone else report the event; (4) **Visual clues** — hand anatomy, lighting and shadow, eye reflections, hair strands vs. clumps, background distortion, garbled text. Clues are the *weakest* check: current generators make them less reliable every year, and the same "clue" appears in compression artefacts and real photographs. |
| 12-22 min | Guided Practice: "Forensic Analysis" | Students receive a worksheet with 6 images. For each they must record the strongest check available, the visual clues they can see, and one line on *what would change their mind*. Teacher reviews as a class, marking down any claim that rests only on visual clues. |
| 22-32 min | Computer Lab Activity: "Reverse Image Search" | Students use Google Images or TinEye to reverse-search the same images. They document where each first appeared. Key lesson: no earlier match does **not** prove an image is fake — a genuinely new photo has no match either. |
| 32-50 min | Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket | Students write: "Which check would you trust most, and what can it *not* tell you?" |

**Materials:** Projector, sample images (printed), annotation worksheets, tablets/computers with internet access

#### Session 5: "Audio Forensics" — Cloned Voice & Lip-Sync Errors (50 min)

**Objective:** Explain why audio and video "tells" are unreliable, and apply source verification to media

| Time | Activity | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| 0-5 min | Hook | Play a video of a celebrity speaking with slightly off lip movements. Ask students to spot the mismatch. Then ask the harder question: *if the lip-sync were perfect, what would you check next?* |
| 5-12 min | Direct Instruction | Teach the clues and their limits: (1) Unnatural breathing pauses, (2) flattened delivery in emotional passages, (3) background noise that does not match the room, (4) lip-sync drift. These are real but fading — a careful clone shows none of them. The reliable check is whether the clip can be traced to a source that published it independently. |
| 12-22 min | Guided Practice: "Audio Detective" | Students receive labelled clips from real and AI-generated speakers. They describe differences in timing, tone, and breathing — then mark each description as *strong* or *weak* evidence, and write what would settle it. |
| 22-32 min | Computer Lab Activity: "Detector Demo and Its Limits" | If a public detector demo is available (e.g. Deepware or Hive), run one clip through it. Then discuss the output honestly: detectors are probabilistic, produce false positives and false negatives, and cannot say *why*. A "94% AI" score is a prompt to check provenance, not a verdict. If no detector is available, run the provenance exercise instead: locate the original upload and compare uploader history. |
| 32-50 min | Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket | Students write: "Why is 'the detector said 94% AI' not proof? What would you do before you shared the clip?" |

**Materials:** Projector, sample clips, annotated audio examples, tablets/computers with internet; a public detector demo is optional, never required

#### Session 6: "Sextortion Syndicates" — The Business Behind the Scam (50 min)

**Objective:** Analyze how sextortion rings operate as organized businesses

| Time | Activity | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| 0-5 min | Hook | Show a flowchart of a typical sextortion ring: Fake profile → Chat → Video → Screenshot → Demand $10-$50 → Block → Repeat. Discuss the profit model. |
| 5-12 min | Direct Instruction | Explain that sextortion is run by professional syndicates, not random hackers. They use fake profiles (often AI-generated avatars), mass-message hundreds of targets daily, and accept crypto payments for anonymity. |
| 12-22 min | Case Study Analysis: "Real Syndicate Reports" | Students read simplified case studies from Cybertip.ca and analyze patterns: average payout, most common apps used, success rate of demands. |
| 22-32 min | Group Activity: "Build a Syndicate Profile" | In groups, students create a profile of a typical sextortion syndicate: target age range, preferred platforms, payment methods, average daily messages sent, success rate. |
| 32-50 min | Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket | Students write: "Why do you think sextortion targets teens specifically? What's one thing that would make the scam harder to pull off?" |

**Materials:** Projector, flowchart handout, case study packets (printed), blank paper for syndicate profiles, timers

#### Session 7: "The 3 Do Nots Deep Dive" — Sextortion Protocols in Action (50 min)

**Objective:** Apply the three core rules of sextortion response to complex scenarios

| Time | Activity | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| 0-5 min | Hook | Review the 3 Do Nots: Don't pay/negotiate, don't delete evidence, don't panic alone. Ask students why each rule matters with real examples. |
| 5-12 min | Direct Instruction | Explain the psychology behind each rule: (1) Paying leads to more demands — the scam is a business, not a one-time event, (2) Deleting evidence loses the username and message timestamps, (3) Panic leads to impulsive decisions like paying immediately. |
| 12-22 min | Scenario Workshop: "Complex Cases" | Students receive advanced scenarios: (1) Scammer threatens to post video at 6 PM but it's already 5:58, (2) Scammer asks for $50 via crypto wallet, (3) Scammer sends a screenshot of the student with a friend's face. Groups decide which Do Nots apply and why. |
| 22-32 min | Group Presentations | Each group presents one scenario and their reasoning. Teacher facilitates discussion on edge cases and trade-offs. |
| 32-50 min | Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket | Students write: "Which rule is hardest to follow in the moment? Why? Give one example where you might break a rule intentionally." |

**Materials:** Projector, scenario workshop packets (printed), blank paper for analysis, timers

#### Session 8: "Crypto Scam Basics" — Rug Pulls, Airdrops & Pump-and-Dump (50 min)

**Objective:** Identify common crypto scam patterns and evaluate project legitimacy

| Time | Activity | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| 0-5 min | Hook | Show a headline: "Teen Loses $2,000 on 'Guaranteed' Crypto Investment." Ask what clues might have warned them. |
| 5-12 min | Direct Instruction | Teach the crypto scam checklist: (1) Guaranteed returns, (2) Pressure to act fast, (3) Unknown wallet addresses, (4) Too-good-to-be-true airdrops, (5) Influencer endorsements without disclosure. Use real examples. |
| 12-22 min | Guided Practice: "Scam or Legit?" | Students receive worksheets with 6 crypto project descriptions. They evaluate each against the checklist and rate legitimacy (1-5). Teacher reviews as a class. |
| 22-32 min | Group Activity: "Build a Scam Project" | In groups, students invent a crypto project using scam elements: catchy name, guaranteed returns, influencer endorsement, urgency countdown. Groups swap and evaluate each other's projects. |
| 32-50 min | Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket | Students write: "What's the most common scam pattern you've seen? Why do people fall for 'guaranteed' returns?" |

**Materials:** Projector, sample crypto project descriptions (printed), evaluation worksheets, blank paper for creating projects, timers

#### Session 9: "Social Media Algorithms" — Echo Chambers & Engagement Bait (50 min)

**Objective:** Understand how platforms monetize attention and identify algorithmic manipulation

| Time | Activity | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| 0-5 min | Hook | Show two TikTok/Reels feeds: one curated, one random. Ask which feels more "personal." Discuss the illusion of choice. |
| 5-12 min | Direct Instruction | Explain algorithmic bias: platforms show content that keeps you engaged (likes, comments, shares), not necessarily what's true or balanced. Introduce engagement bait ("Tag someone who..."). |
| 12-22 min | Guided Practice: "Feed Audit" | Students audit their own social media feeds for the past week. They categorize posts by type (educational, entertainment, sales, opinion) and note patterns in what gets promoted. |
| 22-32 min | Group Activity: "Create Your Own Algorithm" | In groups, students design a simple algorithm that prioritizes engagement over accuracy. They simulate how it would curate a feed of 10 sample posts. |
| 32-50 min | Wrap-Up & Exit Ticket | Students write: "What's one way your feed is biased? How can you 'trick' the algorithm into showing you more diverse content?" |

**Materials:** Projector, sample social media screenshots, audit worksheets, blank paper for algorithm design, timers

#### Session 10: "The Wild West Simulation" — Multi-Platform Scenario (50 min)

**Objective:** Apply all learned skills in a simulated full-day digital experience

### 🔑 Teacher Tips

- **Normalize crypto volatility:** Emphasize that losing money on crypto is normal, but losing it to a scam is preventable.
- **Prepare for varied tech levels:** Some students may be more experienced with certain platforms. Pair stronger students with those needing support.
- **Encourage critical thinking:** Don't just teach "what" — teach "why." Why does the algorithm show this? Why does the scammer use crypto?
- **Connect to careers:** Highlight cybersecurity jobs, data science, UX design, and digital marketing as paths that build on these skills.
- **Parent communication:** Send home a one-page summary of topics covered and suggest family conversations (e.g., "What's our safe word? What crypto apps do we use?").
- **Stop & Think reinforcement:** Each session opens with a "Pause Protocol" — e.g., "Before you invest, breathe. Before you click, check. Before you share, ask yourself: Can I undo this?" The "Urgency Test": If someone says 'Act NOW!' — you have 10 seconds to decide, not 2.
- **Ask for help culture:** "If you struggle with any concept, any assignment, or any screen — speak up. The best cybersecurity pros I know are the ones who ask questions."
- **Break protocol:** High schoolers mask fatigue. If eyes look glassy or attention drifts for more than a lesson: "Brain dump — 3-minute reset. Water. Stretch. Fresh air. You'll retain more coming back refreshed than pushing through fuzzy."

### 📎 Appendix: Sample Simulation Scenario Packet

**Morning — Email Audit:**
> From: supprt@discord.com  
> Subject: Verify your account or lose your Nitro! Click here NOW!  
> [Link: discord-verify-login.com]  
> *Action:* Identify phishing clues. Hover to preview destination. Decide: click, ignore, or report?

**Midday — Voice Call:**
> Mom calls: "Hey sweetie, it's me! I'm at the airport and need $50 for the car. Can you Venmo me?"  
> *Action:* Use safe word ("What's for dinner?"). Verify response matches known answer. Decide: send money or wait?

**Afternoon — Social Media Scroll:**
> A celebrity posts a selfie with 4 fingers on one hand and 6 on the other. Caption: "New photoshoot! 💫"  
> *Action:* Apply deepfake checklist. Use reverse image search. Decide: share, comment, or scroll past?

**Evening — Crypto Investment:**
> Influencer promotes "MoonRocket Token" with guaranteed 500% returns in 30 days. Link to buy: moonrocket-token.com  
> *Action:* Evaluate against scam checklist. Check for unknown wallet, urgency, guaranteed returns. Decide: invest, wait, or skip?

**Night — Sextortion Response:**
> Stranger in Discord sent video of student doing a handstand. Message: "Nice! Send $10 via crypto wallet 0xAbC... or I'll post it at 6 PM."  
> *Action:* Apply the 3 Do Nots + Tell — don't pay, don't reply, don't delete, tell. Decide: pay, reply, or preserve and report?

*Answers:* (1) Phishing — misspelled sender + urgency + suspicious link → report, (2) Voice clone — use safe word → verify before sending, (3) Deepfake — unnatural hands are a clue, not proof → verify the source before acting, (4) Crypto scam — guaranteed returns + unknown wallet → wait/skip, (5) Sextortion — apply the 3 Do Nots + Tell → don't pay, don't reply, don't delete, tell.
